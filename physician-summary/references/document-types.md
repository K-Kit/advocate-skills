# Document-specific selection and research basis

Read the section matching the document. These are selection priorities, not mandatory headings or a request to reproduce every checklist item. Include only what is relevant and supported by the supplied material.

## Clinical records, referrals, and diagnostic reports

Build a compact problem representation from the documented demographics, relevant comorbidities, presentation, and course. Preserve decisive examination, laboratory, imaging, pathology, and microbiology findings; use dates and trends when a single value would mislead. Retain qualifications in diagnostic impressions and distinguish a differential from the final diagnosis.

In the detailed version, organize by active problem or chronology, whichever best explains the case. Include documented treatment changes and response, consequential allergies, pending tests, follow-up, and contingencies. Preserve the assigned clinician/team and due date when specified; if a consequential pending item lacks an owner or timeframe, say so briefly. Do not create a follow-up plan to complete a template.

**Research basis:** [AHRQ TeamSTEPPS: I-PASS](https://www.ahrq.gov/teamstepps-program/curriculum/communication/tools/ipass.html) emphasizes acuity, patient course, assigned actions, timing, and contingencies for handoffs. These inform selection for a written summary; a summary does not itself perform the interactive receiver synthesis required by a handoff.

## Primary research

Anchor the summary in design, population, sample size, intervention/exposure, comparator, primary endpoint, and follow-up. Prioritize the primary result, its effect measure and CI, clinically important harms, and the limitation that most affects interpretation. Use absolute event rates alongside relative effects when reported and useful; preserve which arm each result belongs to.

The detailed version can add eligibility, analysis population, missing data, relevant secondary outcomes, and applicability. Distinguish prespecified from exploratory/post hoc results, adjusted from unadjusted estimates, and surrogate from patient-important outcomes. For observational work, describe association and retain relevant confounding limitations. For noninferiority/equivalence studies, retain the stated margin and analysis when necessary to interpret the conclusion. Do not generalize findings beyond the studied population or treat a research result as an instruction to prescribe.

**Research basis:** [CONSORT 2025 statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11995449/), especially items 14, 21, and 26–30, supports preserving endpoint definitions, analysis populations, effect estimates/precision, harms, and limitations. It is a trial reporting guideline, not a quality score or a validated summarization algorithm.

## Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Retain the review question, included study designs, number of studies/participants, search end date when reported, principal outcome estimates with CIs, and relevant heterogeneity. Carry forward reported risk of bias and evidence certainty, plus limitations such as imprecision or inconsistency. Distinguish pooled findings from narrative synthesis; do not imply all included studies contributed to every outcome. An unreported certainty assessment is not automatically “low certainty.”

**Research basis:** [PRISMA 2020 for Abstracts checklist](https://www.prisma-statement.org/s/PRISMA_2020_abstract_checklist-tg6j.pdf), particularly items 7–10, identifies study counts, synthesis results, evidence limitations, and interpretation as core abstract content.

## Guidelines and consensus statements

Identify the issuing body, version/date, and target population. Preserve the operative recommendation, eligibility/thresholds, exceptions, contraindications, and implementation details that change its application. Attribute recommendations to the document and keep “recommend,” “suggest,” and “consider” distinct. Preserve the source's exact grading system when reported; do not translate between grading systems or assign a grade yourself.

Keep recommendation strength separate from evidence certainty. A strong recommendation does not necessarily mean high-certainty evidence, and low-certainty evidence does not by itself establish a weak recommendation. Do not label a supplied historical guideline “current” without a separately requested verification.

**Research basis:** [GRADE Working Group: Criteria for applying or using GRADE](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/docs/Criteria_for_using_GRADE_2016-04-05.pdf) distinguishes evidence certainty from recommendation strength and calls for transparent judgments supporting each.

## Technical language and medication notation

Use clinical terminology to preserve meaning efficiently; expand shorthand whenever ambiguity could alter care. For example, a medication list stating “D/C” without enough context cannot safely be converted to either “discharge” or “discontinue.” Report the unresolved wording instead of selecting a meaning. Common clinical abbreviations do not require a glossary for physicians, but familiarity never justifies unsafe dose notation.

**Research basis:** [ISMP's 2024 error-prone abbreviation list](https://www.ismp.org/system/files/resources/2024-04/ISMP_ErrorProneAbbreviation_List.pdf) documents harmful misinterpretations and safer forms. [The Joint Commission's terminology and dose-designation guidance](https://www.jointcommission.org/en-us/knowledge-library/support-center/standards-interpretation/standards-faqs/000001229) specifies prohibited medication shorthand and distinguishes dose formatting from precision in laboratory/imaging results.
